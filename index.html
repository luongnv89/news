Coding & agents New

Anthropic says Claude agents helped move its Messages API from Python to Rust on live traffic, cutting CPU per request by about 70%

Published: 25 Nov 2026

Anthropic's engineering team published an account on Wednesday of how it moved its Messages API, the main way customers and its own apps such as Claude Code and Cowork reach Claude, from Python to Rust without taking the service offline. It says API traffic grew roughly 1,800 times from early 2024 to autumn 2026, and that the Rust version uses about 70% less CPU per request and cuts median time to first token by about 20%.

Claude wrote a first version of the core request path in about a week, the post says, but verification was the hard part: in April the Rust API passed about 98% of its tests while only about a quarter of real inputs matched on production traffic. Anthropic shadowed live requests through both versions, turned each difference into a small record and dispatched agents to fix it, using an inner sandbox loop, an outer loop on shadowed traffic and a memory loop that turned engineers' review corrections into rules, tests and lints.

The share of matching inputs fell to 6.5% in early May and reached 99.6% by August, according to the post; public traffic moved to Rust in September. Merged changes to the API's Rust code rose to more than 3,000 per two-week period, not all of them migration work.

Analysis — why it matters. Analysis: the post argues that checking behaviour on production traffic, not writing the code, is what limits agent-driven rewrites of live systems; other teams can test that claim on their own migrations.

Caveat. All figures are Anthropic's own, taken from the text and charts of its post; no outside party has checked them.

UPDATE: Researchers working with Anthropic post a far shorter Lean proof of the Riemann hypothesis and its generalisation for Dirichlet L-functions, so far checked only by machine

Published: 25 Nov 2026, 21:38 CET

Update to the 22 November morning edition, which reported the Lean and Mathlib communities certifying Levent Alpöge's Lean proof of the Riemann hypothesis. The new development: on Wednesday at 21:38 Paris time the GitHub user lsernau pushed grh-lean, a Lean 4 proof of the Riemann hypothesis and of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions. Its README says the proof is based on Alpöge's and is a shorter version 'produced in collaboration with my colleagues at Anthropic, and Claude'.

The repository holds two editions of the same five theorems: a readable one of about 300,000 lines, with a guide, and a compact one of about 76,000 lines. Both build on Mathlib using only Lean's three standard axioms; a build takes one to two hours on 12 cores with 24 GB of memory. The code is licensed Apache 2.0 under an Anthropic copyright.

Separately, the complexity theorist Scott Aaronson wrote on Wednesday that his working assumption is now that 'any proof that comes directly from OpenAI or Anthropic is correct barring information to the contrary', citing the Lean certificates the labs publish. He added that he is 'virtually certain, from multiple reliable sources' that AI labs are sitting on unreleased results that would break currently deployed cryptosystems, while saying he has seen no details and is much less confident about their nature. No lab has published such a result.

Analysis — why it matters. Analysis: a readable edition a few hundred thousand lines long makes a human read-through more feasible, and that read-through is the step the proof still lacks.

Caveat. The README says the proof has passed machine checks only: no mathematician has read it through and no paper comes with it. The repository sits on a personal GitHub account, not Anthropic's, and Anthropic has made no announcement. Aaronson's cryptography remarks are second-hand and, by his own account, unverified.

Industry & policy New

FCC authorises SpaceX to deploy up to 1 million 'Starmind' data-centre satellites, the first US approval of an orbital compute constellation

Published: 25 Nov 2026

The US Federal Communications Commission said on Wednesday that it had granted SpaceX authorisation to deploy up to 1,000,000 orbital compute modules, the 'Starmind' constellation, and called it the first authorisation in US history for a constellation of data centres in space. The FCC's release says the system operates consistently with its orbital-debris rules and that the agency is still reviewing other orbital data-centre proposals.

Chairman Brendan Carr framed the grant around what the release calls America's super intelligence economy. Besides the constellation itself, the authorisation covers radio links for backup telemetry, tracking and command, and radio backup for the optical inter-satellite network in the 18.8 to 19.3 GHz downlink and 28.6 to 29.1 GHz uplink bands.

Ars Technica, which describes the grant as conditional, reports that SpaceX has seven years to deploy a single operational Starmind satellite, until November 2035 for 100,000 and until November 2040 for the full constellation. It says SpaceX proposed six orbits from 550 km to about 1,000 km and sought a waiver to move retired satellites above 600 km into disposal or heliocentric orbits instead of burning them up. The Verge notes that SpaceX says Starmind will send data to Earth over laser links to Starlink.

Analysis — why it matters. Analysis: the approval clears a regulatory hurdle, not the technical or economic ones; Ars notes that it does not establish that orbital data centres are viable. If fully built, Ars says, the constellation would raise the number of satellites in orbit roughly 50-fold, so the conditions on orbits and disposal carry real weight.

Caveat. The deployment deadlines, orbits and waiver request come from Ars Technica's reading of the order; the FCC's two-page release gives none of them. SpaceX's description of Starmind is its own claim.

UPDATE: S&P puts Nvidia's AA credit rating on watch for a possible one-notch cut, citing board independence and 'key man' risk

Published: 25 Nov 2026, 19:38 CET

Update to the 23 November afternoon edition, which reported Nvidia's board reinstating Jensen Huang as chief executive, and the 24 November morning edition, which reported Commerce adding Megaspeed and six other Asian firms to the Entity List. The new development: on Wednesday at 13:38 New York time S&P Global Ratings placed Nvidia's AA issuer and senior unsecured ratings on CreditWatch negative.

S&P said recent, well-publicised developments had raised questions about Nvidia's management and governance, including board independence and key man risk, and that it was unaware of any remedial action by the board. It said the long tenure of certain board members 'may have compromised their independence', and that the CEO's departure and reinstatement 'does not sufficiently answer our questions regarding board oversight of executive code of conduct'. It revised its governance modifier to neutral from positive.

S&P expects to resolve the review within 90 days and to limit any downgrade to one notch, unless investigations or disclosures lead it to believe Nvidia's reputation or prospects have been harmed. It said Nvidia's competitive position and finances are unchanged, but that evidence of circumvented export rules or misleading China disclosures would weigh on its view. TechCrunch notes that one notch would take Nvidia to AA-, and that a lower rating could raise costs for the neoclouds and infrastructure companies whose debt Nvidia backstops.

Analysis — why it matters. Analysis: a credit rating agency is now putting a price on the governance questions raised by the spy and chip-smuggling reports, at a company whose guarantees support part of the AI data-centre build-out.

Caveat. A CreditWatch placement is not a downgrade; S&P says it will meet the board and management first. The possible effect on borrowers Nvidia backstops is TechCrunch's analysis, not S&P's. S&P's page refused a plain request (HTTP 403) and was read with a web-extraction tool.

Industry & policy Updated

UPDATE: Microsoft says China's Salt Typhoon and Crystal Typhoon and two North Korean groups have added the ARTEX agentic attack framework to their tooling

Published: 25 Nov 2026, 19:09 CET

Update to the 22 October morning edition, which reported Microsoft's finding that the China-aligned group Storm-0940 used ARTEX against hundreds of organisations; later editions carried CrowdStrike's VAPOR PANDA report (30 October), a Japan-led probe of ARTEX servers (15 November) and the European Commission's AI-assisted breach (19 November). The new development: on Wednesday evening Microsoft Threat Intelligence said that, as of November, it has seen Salt Typhoon and Crystal Typhoon, North Korea's Onyx Sleet and Pearl Sleet, and the groups Storm-0940 and Storm-3358 integrating ARTEX into their tooling.

Microsoft describes ARTEX as an AI-driven penetration-testing platform in which a planner agent, worker agents with a real shell and a reporter agent take an attack from reconnaissance to exploitation with little or no human involvement. It says the tool introduces no new techniques but can reach persistent, privileged access in minutes, and that the most durable sign of it is behavioural: bursts of varied requests and retries at machine speed.

According to Microsoft, the original developer removed the code from GitHub in October, but forks continue, and in November the Chinese security firm ThreatBook claimed ownership and began selling 'ARTEX Pro'. The Record notes that Salt Typhoon is the group US agencies believe breached at least nine telecommunications companies in 2024, and that the FBI issued its own ARTEX advisory two weeks ago.

Analysis — why it matters. Analysis: the list now includes the state group blamed for the 2024 US telecom breaches, which moves agentic attack tooling from a criminal problem to a state-espionage one.

Caveat. Attribution to named groups is Microsoft's own, and it gives no detail on how each group used the tool. ThreatBook's ownership claim is reported by Microsoft.
