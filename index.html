The News

Models New

Cognition releases SWE-2 Flash, a cheaper coding model for Devin, and keeps its weights behind a vetting programme

Published: 4 Nov 2026, 19:00 CET

Cognition, the maker of the Devin coding agent, released SWE-2 Flash on Wednesday in Devin Desktop and the Devin CLI. It says the model runs at one-fifth the price and three times the speed of its larger SWE-2, and its launch page lists it as free through 30 November.

SWE-2 Flash is built on Thinking Machines' open-weight Inkling-Small, which has 276 billion total and 12 billion active parameters. Cognition added a mid-training stage before reinforcement learning and says this raised Terminal-Bench 4.0 from 0.9% to 20.0% and FrontierCode 2.0 from 3.2% to 24.0% over the base model, and that SWE-2 Flash beats all American open-weight models on leading evaluations.

Cognition also trained the model for security work, lifting its ExploitBench score from 11.3% to 34.8% by its account. Citing cyber risk, it is not publishing the weights and is building a programme to share them with trusted industry partners and research labs, with details promised in a few weeks.

Analysis — why it matters. Analysis: Coding-agent companies are post-training open bases to cut the cost of routine work instead of renting frontier models for every task. The gated weights also show cyber risk starting to decide who gets model weights.

Caveat. All benchmark figures are Cognition's own, and its comparison covers only American open-weight models. The weight-sharing programme has not been described in detail.

Models New

OpenAI releases Images 2.6, adding animated GIFs and two new image models to its API

Published: 4 Nov 2026

OpenAI released Images 2.6 in ChatGPT, Codex and the API on Wednesday. It says the update generates images 50% faster and adds animated GIFs, and that people now create 4 billion images a week across its products.

In the API the release ships as two models, according to OpenAI's changelog: GPT-Image-2.6 Flare for fast iteration and GPT-Image-2.6 Sunburst for detail and consistency across edits. Developers also get markup-guided edits, SVG output and a complexity setting that controls how much planning the model does.

OpenAI's system card says GIFs bring risks that single images do not, such as harmful meaning conveyed across frames, and that motion-related violations remain challenging to detect.

Analysis — why it matters. Analysis: An incremental release, but GIF output in the API moves OpenAI's image models a step toward short animation, with new moderation problems attached.

Caveat. Speed and usage figures are OpenAI's. The release was announced on 4 November before the previous edition went to press but was not carried in it.

Coding & agents New

Meta buys Conductor, maker of an app for running coding agents in parallel, and its cloud service closes on 20 November

Published: 4 Nov 2026

Meta has acquired Conductor, a start-up whose Mac app lets developers run several AI coding agents at once in separate copies of a project, Axios reported on Wednesday. Meta confirmed the deal to TechCrunch but gave no further details, and terms were not disclosed.

Conductor said it has stopped new sign-ups and downloads. Conductor Cloud goes read-only on 13 November and shuts down on 20 November, when the company begins deleting cloud user data; active subscribers get refunds. The local app will keep receiving new models and critical fixes until early 2027, and co-founder Charlie Holtz promised something new to use early next year.

Axios set the deal beside OpenAI's planned purchase of Pierre Computer, announced the same day and covered in the previous edition. TechCrunch notes that Conductor runs agents from labs such as Anthropic and OpenAI rather than its own.

Analysis — why it matters. Analysis: Two large labs bought developer-tool start-ups on the same day. The place where developers supervise many agents at once now looks as contested as the models behind them.

Caveat. Meta has not said how it will use Conductor's team or product. The Hacker News reactions below are anecdotal.

Research New

OpenAI says an unreleased cyber model found and exploited 948 Linux kernel flaws in one $1.2 million run

Published: 4 Nov 2026

OpenAI published research on Wednesday on an automated pipeline that, in a mid-October run with an upcoming cyber model, analysed 2,308 candidate Linux kernel findings, reproduced 1,724 vulnerabilities and produced 948 local privilege escalation exploits. Such flaws let an unprivileged local user gain root. The run cost $1,213,430 in API credits, or $1,279 per exploit, OpenAI said.

The pipeline has five stages: discovery, validation in isolated virtual machines, exploitability testing, patch generation and human review before anything reaches maintainers. OpenAI said the targets ran a stock desktop operating system with standard protections, that it began with critical bugs on the Linux community's guidance, and that it is sending further reports and patches with its Patch the Planet partners.

OpenAI said the October model found more than five times as many novel exploits as a GPT-5.6 Sol run in July, and that its agents used at least 10 exploitation techniques new to outside kernel experts; it withheld their details. In an early run, 20 of 200 reported successes reached root through a different zero-day than the one assigned, which OpenAI cites as a reason to verify how agents reach a result.

Analysis — why it matters. Analysis: Finding and weaponising kernel bugs is turning into a compute bill rather than a staffing problem. OpenAI's own advice is that a low-privileged account on an unpatched system should be treated as equivalent to root for a well-resourced attacker.

Caveat. These are OpenAI's figures for an unnamed, unreleased model, not independently audited. The 948 counts distinct candidate patches, not merged fixes; OpenAI said over 100 of the flaws were patched or had staging patches from other contributors within 10 days. The exploits are local, not remote.

Industry & policy New

Brockman says most of OpenAI's revenue now comes from businesses, and that it could be ready to go public in 2027

Published: 4 Nov 2026

OpenAI President Greg Brockman said on Wednesday that the company now generates the majority of its revenue from enterprise sales, Reuters reported from a Wall Street Journal technology conference in Napa, California. Asked on stage whether OpenAI would be ready for an IPO next year, he said: "I think so."

According to Reuters, Brockman said OpenAI has been ramping up sales to businesses, including its Codex agent, in competition with Anthropic, which Reuters said is likely to go public before the end of the year.

Analysis — why it matters. Analysis: With Anthropic's listing expected within weeks, OpenAI is now presenting itself as a business-software company. A 2027 listing is Brockman's expectation, not a filing or a date.

Caveat. Brockman gave no figures, and Reuters' report does not say how the revenue share is measured.

Industry & policy New

Pentagon technology chief says the US will set out the laws that apply to AI labs within weeks

Published: 5 Nov 2026, 00:42 CET

The US government will lay out all the laws and regulations that apply to domestic AI labs later this month, Pentagon Chief Technology Officer Emil Michael said on Wednesday, Reuters reported. Michael, vice chair of the government's Super Intelligence Task Force, said the group would publicise the regulatory landscape "in the next week or two".

Speaking at the Wall Street Journal conference in Napa, he gave no further details. "What we're trying to do is create a framework that makes the American people have confidence that these companies are going to do the right thing," he said. "Or else I think what you get is 535 legislators writing a piece of regulation that could kill the industry."

Analysis — why it matters. Analysis: Michael describes a map of existing law, presented as an alternative to new legislation from Congress. It comes the day after midterm votes in which local voters backed limits on data centres, covered in the previous edition.

Caveat. This is a stated plan, not a published document, and Reuters reports nothing about what the list will contain.

Industry & policy New

Anthropic struck its first news-licensing deal, with the AP, before the midterms, Adweek reports

Published: 5 Nov 2026, 01:00 CET

Anthropic signed a content and data licensing deal with the Associated Press just before the US midterm elections, Adweek reported, citing two people familiar with the matter. According to Adweek, Claude users who asked for updates on election night saw up-to-date information attributed to the AP.

Adweek says this is the first time Anthropic has licensed content from any digital publisher, while OpenAI has negotiated more than a dozen such deals. Financial terms and duration were not disclosed; Anthropic did not respond to Adweek's request for comment and the AP gave no comment on the record.

Analysis — why it matters. Analysis: Live answers on events such as elections depend on publishers that block AI crawlers. Adweek itself cautions that one deal does not show a lasting change in Anthropic's licensing strategy.

Caveat. Neither company has confirmed the deal on the record; the report rests on anonymous sources. Its scope, including whether it covers model training, is unknown.

Industry & policy New

Apple cuts some engineers' daily AI budgets from $300 to $200 and plans deeper cuts, Bloomberg reports

Published: 4 Nov 2026

Apple lowered the daily AI spending cap for some engineers from $300 to $200 last month and plans further cuts that could take some engineering teams to $100 a day, Bloomberg reported on Wednesday, according to The Next Web and 9to5Mac. Many staff outside engineering already have a $100 cap, which Apple has considered cutting to as little as $25 for some teams; employees can ask senior leaders for exemptions.

The budgets cover tokens used with tools such as Anthropic's Claude, OpenAI's ChatGPT and Google's Gemini, and some teams are being told to switch to cheaper or on-device models. Apple declined to comment, The Next Web says, and notes that Apple's research and development costs rose 32% in its 2026 fiscal year, partly on infrastructure and AI costs.

Analysis — why it matters. Analysis: After urging heavy use, large employers are starting to budget AI tokens like any other expense. Goldman Sachs' limits on AI coding tools, covered earlier, were a similar move.

Caveat. Bloomberg's report rests on unnamed people and was not read directly (paywalled); this dispatch relies on The Next Web's and 9to5Mac's accounts. It is unclear how much Apple will save.
