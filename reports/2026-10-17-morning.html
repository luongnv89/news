Coding & agents New

Claude Code 2.1.302 limits image reads and changes default permission behavior

Published: 16 Oct 2026, 22:53 CEST

Anthropic published stable Claude Code 2.1.302 on October 16 at 22:53 CEST. The release fixes the Read tool loading arbitrarily large images, or pipes and devices named like images, entirely into memory. Image sources larger than 128 MB are now refused.

The changelog says headless claude -p and Agent SDK sessions without an explicit permission mode now start in default mode on third-party providers, with telemetry off or on a fresh install; explicitly selecting auto mode keeps it. A separate setting, CLAUDE_CODE_AUTO_MODE_MODEL, chooses the client-side auto-mode classifier model and does not change the server-side classifier.

The release also fixes MCP servers returning no tools or failing calls when negotiating protocol version 2026-07-28 without resultType, ttlMs or cacheScope. It fixes cloud-session scheduled runs unexpectedly restarting an hour or more after a partial failure, a behavior the changelog says could repeat work such as posting a report twice.

Analysis — why it matters. Operators of unattended agents should review explicit permission settings and duplicate-action protections. Input-size limits and clearer failure states matter independently of model capability.

Caveat. These are vendor-documented changes, not a security audit or an installation recommendation. We verified the official release metadata and notes; we did not install the version or reproduce every fix. Codex 0.164.0 builds found this morning are prereleases with sparse notes, not a verified stable feature launch.

Research New

Claude reconstructs NIST curve-seed origins, not an ECDSA vulnerability

Published: 16 Oct 2026, 19:02 CEST

Anthropic published a reproducible explanation for the seeds behind the NIST P-192, P-224, P-256, P-384 and P-521 elliptic curves. Its internal research model identified output from the C-library lrand48 pseudorandom generator initialized with Unix timestamps, rather than the rumored hash of a humorous passphrase. The article's publication timestamp is October 16 at 19:02 CEST, after the previous edition.

The company says the initial discovery took under half an hour. Its article includes code for regenerating the seeds and explains some selected base points. For this edition, an independent local implementation of the published timestamp-and-generator recipe reproduced the exact standardized P-256 seed. That is a narrow computational check, not an independent reconstruction of the whole investigation.

Anthropic explicitly says this is not a cryptographic break, a new attack or a reason to change ECDSA deployments. It argues the simple generation method reduces concerns about deliberately selected weaknesses. Important questions remain: B-163's seed is unexplained, several base-point origins remain unknown, and the reason some apparently viable curve candidates were skipped is not established.

Analysis — why it matters. A transparent, reproducible origin story helps distinguish parameter-provenance concerns from actual cryptographic vulnerabilities. Security teams should read the result as new evidence about historical choices, not as an emergency migration advisory.

Caveat. The investigation and broader security interpretation are Anthropic's. Our local verification reproduced only P-256's seed. The findings do not constitute a proof that every curve-selection decision was innocuous, nor do they recover private keys.

Research New

Anthropic reports new quantum-circuit designs, including a 17-fold gate reduction

Published: 16 Oct 2026, 19:29 CEST

Anthropic's October 16 research announcement, published at 19:29 CEST, describes evaluator-guided quantum-circuit design using Claude Mythos 5. The model received plain-language specifications, proposed implementations, and iterated against a program that checked correctness and scored resource use across eight computational primitives.

The associated preprint was submitted to arXiv on October 15 at 17:59 UTC. Its abstract reports reductions in both Toffoli gates and qubits on seven tasks; the eighth produced a new reversible architecture for prime-field modular inversion. This edition treats the October 16 announcement as the new event and preserves the paper's earlier submission date.

In one design trade-off, the announcement reports 17 times fewer gates while using 12% more qubits than the smallest previous design. For a Reed-Solomon decoder used in decoded quantum interferometry, it reports a 12-fold gate reduction relative to a previous Google construction. These are specific circuit-resource comparisons, not a claim that all quantum algorithms are 17 times faster.

The modular-inversion search ran for 53 hours with an initial brief and occasional human prompts to broaden its approach. The decoder reserves less storage by accepting rare failures, about one in 10,000 sampled cases under the stated allowance. An external evaluator here means a separate checking program, not independent replication by another research group.

Analysis — why it matters. Verifiable code and explicit resource objectives can make a well-defined scientific optimization task suitable for an iterative agent. Lower circuit costs could help future hardware, but do not themselves demonstrate useful quantum advantage on today's machines.

Caveat. The paper is a preprint and the numerical results are the authors' reports. We verified the primary announcement, abstract and submission history, not every circuit or the full experimental artifact. Resource trade-offs, allowed failure rates and human interventions matter; this is not evidence of unrestricted autonomous scientific discovery.

Industry & policy New

Anthropic reports six agent-hours per human-hour, alongside a falling junior-hiring share

Published: 16 Oct 2026, 18:26 CEST

An internal employment study published by Anthropic on October 16 at 18:26 CEST says employee-directed interactive agents logged six times the company's estimated human work hours by August. The numerator is agent runtime, including inference and active tool use, pooling subagents and excluding waits longer than 30 minutes. Human hours are estimated from headcount at 40 hours per week. This is not a finding that agents were six times as productive as people.

The study reports that junior employees' share of technical headcount fell from 21% to 14% since early 2025, while juniors' share of technical hires fell from 27% to 9%. Technical hiring still grew in absolute terms as the company expanded. A decline in hiring share is not equivalent to an observed count of jobs eliminated.

To examine coding-tool adoption, the authors follow 192 adopting engineers and compare them with 74 non-adopters. They report rising code output, but acknowledge that adoption was not randomized and that more code does not necessarily mean more business impact. Their code-quality proxies include later deletions and reversions, not a comprehensive independent quality assessment.

The report describes human attention as a bottleneck. One interviewed engineer called the experience 'an army of Claudes, with every one asking for my attention.' It also reports that interactive sessions spent around 80% of wall-clock time waiting for human input. That idle-time measure is distinct from the active agent-hours ratio.

Analysis — why it matters. This is a concrete case study of how agent adoption can shift work toward orchestration, review and judgment. Its measurement definitions are as important as the headline ratios when evaluating staffing or productivity claims.

Caveat. This is a single rapidly growing AI vendor studying itself, with internal data and 17 employee interviews. Selection, revenue growth, organizational change and non-random tool adoption limit causal inference and generalization. We did not access private employee or candidate records, and this reporting makes no changes to Job Alerts.

Research Background — older context

BACKGROUND: Google reports supervised lab-in-the-loop advances, not a fully autonomous wet lab

Published: 16 Oct 2026

BACKGROUND - original publication October 16, 2026. Google Research describes three projects connecting AI agents to biological experiments: the VERID laboratory digital twin and robotics safeguards, closed-loop aptamer discovery, and de novo protein-binder design. The page gives no publication hour we could verify, so this dispatch is not claimed as a development after the previous edition.

For aptamers, the team reports three design-test cycles with final confirmed on-target hit rates of 33.5% for myoglobin and 42.1% for alpha-thrombin. The myoglobin experiment initially got worse when designs favored nonspecific sticky binders, then recovered after revising the design rules. These two targets and experimental cycles do not establish general reliability across biology.

The protein-binder work reports more than an eightfold computational-cycle acceleration and single-digit nanomolar affinity against FAP. Google's next step is to combine the projects: the post does not say the complete integrated autonomous lab already exists. Safeguards include sequence screening, simulated robot runs, human authorization of sequences and protocols, and supervision of execution.

Analysis — why it matters. Real experimental feedback can expose shortcuts that look successful in simulation. The reported failure-and-recovery sequence is more informative about the scientific workflow than an undifferentiated claim that AI has automated a laboratory.

Caveat. These are results reported by Google, not replicated by this newspaper. Tight binding is not proof of a clinically useful drug. Integration remains future work, and the experiments retain human oversight. This older, newly verified context is explicitly separated from the four timed new developments.
