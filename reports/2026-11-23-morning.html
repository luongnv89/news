Models New

India's Sarvam releases Sarvam-105B-1711, an open-weight checkpoint it says matches Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite at under a fifth of the price

Published: 22 Nov 2026

Bengaluru start-up Sarvam released Sarvam-105B-1711 on Sunday 22 November, a new open-weight checkpoint of its 105-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model, which has 10.3 billion active parameters. It is post-trained for tool calling, instruction following, agentic tasks and Indian languages, and the BF16 weights and a 65 GB NVFP4 version are on Hugging Face under the Apache 2.0 licence.

Sarvam says that across eight conversational-agent benchmarks the model averages 73.9, against 74.0 for Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and 73.0 for Gemma 4 31B, at a blended $0.15 per million tokens against $0.85 and $0.54; its February release averaged 42.6. It says the NVFP4 build runs on a single RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU and that the model's context now reaches 160,000 tokens.

Moneycontrol reports that co-founder Pratyush Kumar announced the release while answering criticism that Sarvam is moving from training its own models to selling inference through its new Token Factory, and that he said the company will release several models in vision, speech and edge AI over the next two weeks.

Analysis — why it matters. Analysis: Sarvam is competing on price and on running inside a customer's own premises for Indian-language agent work, rather than on frontier capability.

Caveat. All benchmark scores and prices are Sarvam's own claims from its own evaluation runs, and no independent evaluation was found. The comparison is with small 'Flash-Lite' class models, not frontier models.

Research New

OpenAI's chief scientist proposes industry-wide limits on AI development measured by perplexity, and publishes OpenAI's own pretraining curve

Published: 22 Nov 2026

OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published an essay on Sunday 22 November arguing that model developers should adopt perplexity, or cross-entropy loss, as a shared measure of AI progress. He proposes common limits that developers would commit not to cross 'until our ability to understand and monitor AI behavior achieves a certain concrete milestone', such as a successor to chain-of-thought monitors.

As a first step, the post publishes OpenAI's internal perplexity measurements since GPT-3.5, taken on a held-out dataset drawn from OpenAI's own internal codebase. It puts GPT-3.5 at a log-perplexity of about 0.99 and GPT-4 at about 0.91, says that losses below 0.8 brought 'transformative capabilities in fields like cybersecurity and mathematics', and says a model at about 0.76 began producing 'groundbreaking results' on open mathematics problems.

Pachocki writes that a similar 'avalanche of fundamental pretraining advancements' could come 'as soon as 2027', and that further transformative capabilities should be expected in a 0.68 to 0.7 log-perplexity window. He lists the metric's weaknesses: it does not measure a task anyone cares about directly, chain-of-thought reasoning does not lower it, and nobody knows the data's inherent entropy.

TechCrunch reports that Sam Altman called the post 'the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while', and places it after Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei's call for companies to agree 'common safety standards as well as limits on the rate of unchecked AI progress'.

Analysis — why it matters. Analysis: a pacing agreement needs a number every lab can measure, and OpenAI has now put its own pretraining curve in public to offer one. Whether rival labs publish comparable figures is the test of the idea.

Caveat. This is a proposal in an essay by OpenAI's chief scientist, not an agreement: the post names no other lab that has signed up and sets no threshold. The perplexity figures are OpenAI's own, on a private dataset, so they cannot be checked or compared with other labs' numbers.

Industry & policy Updated

UPDATE: The US spy chief confirms investigations of Alice Huang and Megaspeed, as the NYT reports she was in touch with a Chinese intelligence officer for more than a year

Published: 22 Nov 2026

Update to the 22 November afternoon edition, which reported Saturday's Wall Street Journal and New York Times accounts of the chip network around Megaspeed and a Chinese spy-agency campaign to obtain AI chips. The new development: on Sunday the Times reported that Alice Huang was in touch with an officer of China's Ministry of State Security from spring 2025 until this autumn, citing people with knowledge of US intelligence reports, according to Times Now.

The Times also reported that US intelligence gathered evidence that her business partner, Kang Wei, worked with the same agency, and that advisers told President Trump at least twice, before the scandal became public, about her alleged ties; Trump has said no one told him. Officials are still working out how far that work went, the report says.

Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton said on ABC's This Week and CNN's State of the Union that the Justice Department is investigating Alice Huang and her company, and declined to say more, Bloomberg reports. Asked by CNN to reassure Americans that China did not get Nvidia's advanced chips, he said he expects China has found ways to get hold of them, according to CNN's summary of the interview.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CBS that Nvidia must move quickly on its internal investigation. Bloomberg also reports that Jensen Huang is due to answer lawmakers' questions on 1 December in a closed-door joint session of the House Intelligence, Foreign Affairs and China Select committees.

Analysis — why it matters. Analysis: with the intelligence chief, the Treasury and three House committees now involved, the affair has become a formal test of how US chip export controls are enforced.

Caveat. The New York Times report is paywalled and was not read here; its claims come through Times Now and rest on unnamed officials. No charge against Alice Huang has been reported, and Clayton declined to say whether she spied.

Industry & policy Updated

UPDATE: Microsoft's Brad Smith warns of months more outage risk from the AI-driven patching race, and pushes back on Trump's data-centre tax

Published: 22 Nov 2026

Update to the 19 November morning edition, which reported Satya Nadella's warning that much critical infrastructure is 'unpatchable' as AI finds flaws faster. The new development: Microsoft president Brad Smith told CBS's Face the Nation on Sunday that the recent outages are, 'at least in substantial part', AI ripple effects, because AI made flaws easier to find and set off a race to patch at 'a pace we have never seen before'.

Smith said that patching so fast, especially on older infrastructure and with less time to test changes, raises the risk of failures, and that 'we have to be prepared, frankly, for the coming months'. He said Microsoft's own outage around 12 November was not linked to AI and was not a cyberattack, and that the company has taken nine corrective steps, with 13 more to come.

Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton told CNN that Americans should expect 'bumps in the road' as systems are upgraded, The Verge reports.

On President Trump's threatened 'tremendous tax' on data centres built abroad, reported in the 21 November morning edition, Smith said the president is 'making an important point' about training the most advanced models in the US, but that most data centres serve nearby cloud customers: 'You can't support Helsinki from New Jersey'.

Analysis — why it matters. Analysis: the company whose software runs much of the world's office IT now expects the rush to fix AI-found flaws to keep causing failures for months, so outages become a running cost of AI-assisted vulnerability discovery.

Caveat. Smith's link between AI and the outages is his own assessment, not the finding of an investigation, and he gave no list of which outages he counts as AI ripple effects.
